# Research design and source practice

## Design a useful research brief

Write for a researcher who will return material from which another agent can
learn the field and compose a concise guide. Ask for concrete knowledge and
explanatory connections, not a catalog of papers or a project implementation plan.

Each independently usable prompt should contain:

- **Purpose and scope:** the fields or subtopics, intended reader, motivating
  interests, and boundaries. The goal is reusable foundations, disciplinary
  fluency, and research judgment.
- **Substantive directions:** specific concepts, phenomena, models, or questions
  worth understanding, with the relationships that make them belong together.
- **Source practice:** suitable orientation sources and primary or methods work
  for important claims, following the reading guidance below.
- **Returned report:** a complete, coherent Markdown knowledge report with
  established terminology, concrete explanations, representative examples, and
  traceable sources. Note reading limitations that affect its claims or coverage.

Include relevant prior findings directly in the prompt, or provide the material
with it. Carry over the source and writing expectations rather than referring to
skill files the researcher cannot access. Each prompt should be executable without
waiting for another group's report. Returned reports may be richer than the final
guide; its word budget is not a separate limit for each report.

## Choose directions that teach the field

Select a few productive connections for each research group. For example:

- Connect a phenomenon to the distinctions and observations needed to explain it.
- Explain a theoretical construct's role and how competing accounts differ in
  predictions or interpretation.
- Relate a model's variables, assumptions, and operations to what it explains.
- Connect a measurement or analytic result to its data, units, and comparison.
- Explain how learning, development, history, or scale changes the field's objects
  and questions when that connection is important.

These are ways to choose content, not mandatory sections. Use the field's own
questions and practices to decide what needs explanation, including methodological
foundations where useful. A mathematical demonstration or invented teaching
example should be recognizable as such, rather than presented as an empirical
finding.

Research taste should emerge from the account: why a distinction is useful, what
a well-chosen example reveals, how an abstraction retains what matters, and how
researchers express quantities and comparisons. Avoid repeating a generic caution
checklist after every topic. Let the content determine headings and emphasis.

## Read and resolve sources

Start with reliable orientation: textbooks, substantial reviews, or comparable
field-appropriate syntheses. Use primary studies and methods papers to anchor
important claims, explain influential approaches, or develop informative
contrasts. Include recent developments when they change the conceptual picture.

Retrieve and inspect the source material relevant to the claim: its argument,
methods, results, figures, or limitations. Titles and search snippets provide
leads, not the substance of the research. When a source is inaccessible, seek an
accessible version or another suitable source.

Keep the connection between a claim and its source in working context, including
a DOI or stable link and the relevant passage or section. Distinguish direct
source reading from reliance on an abstract, a supplied report, or a delegate's
account. This does not require a separate source ledger or research archive.

Check consequential conflicts at their sources. Distinguish observations,
authors' interpretations, and your synthesis; preserve uncertainty where it
changes a claim's meaning. Revisit an explanation or research a missing foundation
when doing so would materially improve the integrated guide. Stop when the
central concepts and connections have adequate support. Report an access gap
when it prevents that coverage.
