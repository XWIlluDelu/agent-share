# Field-guide examples

Use these guides to calibrate editorial judgment, explanatory
density, and professional language, or to compare a new draft with a concrete
example. They are reference points, not mandatory outlines or gold-standard
answers. All three concern neuroscience or adjacent fields; Get Taste remains
a general workflow for other disciplines.

## Choose an example

Start with this index and choose a guide for the editorial problem at hand, not
just matching subject words. Do not load all three by default. Read another only
when it helps answer a distinct writing or comparison question.

| Guide | Particularly useful for |
|---|---|
| [Computational neuroscience](computational-neuroscience-field-guide.md) | Organizing a broad field around models, measurements, computation, and evaluation. Notice how terminology and quantitative measures retain their scientific objects and comparisons. |
| [Systems and cognitive neuroscience](systems-cognitive-neuroscience-field-guide.md) | Connecting cognitive phenomena, learning, population dynamics, and analysis. Notice how examples connect concepts across levels rather than becoming a paper-by-paper catalog. |
| [Neural control](neural-control-field-guide.md) | Connecting disciplinary traditions. Notice how states, inputs, observations, and objectives connect theory, algorithms, neural computation, and experiments. |

Borrow the editorial decision, not the domain syllabus. For example, a guide to
materials science might benefit from the neural-control example's explicit
connection between model variables and measurements without adopting neural
state-space chapters or its particular balance of topics.

## Compare quality through passages

Use the current task, audience, available sources, and approved budget as the
standard. The [editing guide](../references/field-guide.md) supplies the general
criteria. A useful comparison asks:

- **Knowledge structure:** Does the draft teach a coherent set of concepts and
  relationships, rather than preserve its reports' order or list subfields?
- **Explanatory value:** Do examples, models, and any equations show how something
  works and why a finding matters? Can the reader follow the relevant operations?
- **Language and judgment:** Are terms, quantities, comparisons, and scope precise
  and natural? Does the content convey research judgment without repeated generic
  warnings?
- **Selection:** Do important connections survive the cuts? Do the remaining
  references support particular claims and offer useful reading entry points?
- **Reuse:** Does the guide explain essential prerequisites and stand on its own
  for a technical collaborator?

When asked for a quality comparison, point to representative passages in the
new draft and the chosen example, explain the consequential editorial difference,
and recommend a few useful revisions. Recognize justified differences in subject,
reader needs, and scope. Do not score similarity of wording or require matching
chapter counts, tables, formulas, reference counts, or exact length.

Factual correctness and citation support still require the relevant sources.
Current user requirements and skill instructions take precedence over incidental
choices in an example.
